Research on career transitions

Ibarra + Barbulescu Found Something Worth Pausing On

Identity Claims Meet a Community

The new story must be understood beyond the person telling it.

Herminia Ibarra and Roxana Barbulescu — in their research on career transitions — found something worth pausing on.

The community decides

Will Others Grant the New Identity?

Your new identity claim
Accepted as legitimate in the new field

They studied whether hiring communities grant someone's new identity claim — in other words, whether people actually accept you as a legitimate member of the new field.

The strongest predictor

Narrative Coherence

A story the listener can grant.

And what they found is that the single strongest predictor of a granted identity is what they call narrative coherence.

A good pivot story has three parts.

Backstory

What You Built and Accomplished

1

Your Credibility Lives Here

Your prior career belongs in the story.

First, the backstory: what you built and accomplished in your prior career. This is where your credibility lives.

Claim the backstory

Do Not Minimize It. Do Not Apologize for It

Claim it.

You don't minimize it. You don't apologize for it. You claim it.

Turning Point

What Changed, Shifted, or Became Possible?

2

Explain the Pivot

Use conviction, not self-deprecation.

Second, the turning point: what changed, shifted, or became possible. This is where you explain the pivot without self-deprecation.

Shift the framing

The Same Pivot Can Tell a Different Story

“I was miserable and had to escape.”
“Three things converged that made this next direction the clear one.”

Notice the shift in framing — not "I was miserable and had to escape," but "three things converged that made this next direction the clear one."

The framing matters

Same Event. Very Different Story

Same event. Very different story.

Forward Case

Why Your Combination Creates Unique Value

3

Claim the Advantage

Your background strengthens the new direction.

Third, the forward case: why your specific combination of background and new direction creates unique value. This is where you claim the advantage your pivot creates.

Finance director → Product manager

Do Not Lead with What You Are Learning

“I'm learning product.”

The finance director turned product manager doesn't lead with "I'm learning product."

Lead with the advantage

“I'm a Product Manager Who Can Read a P&L”

A capability most peers cannot claim.

She leads with "I'm a product manager who can read a P&L like most of my peers can't."

Before you write

Watch for Three Failure Modes

Now watch for three narrative failure modes.

Failure mode one

Apologizing for the Pivot

“I know this seems like a big jump…”
Cut that.

First, apologizing for the pivot. "I know this seems like a big jump…" Cut that.

Why apology fails

Lead with Conviction

The listener needs to believe that you believe the pivot.

It signals a lack of conviction to the very people you need to convince.

Failure mode two

Framing the Old Career as a Mistake

Erase everything that came before.

That framing tanks the story.

Second, framing the old career as a mistake. Even if you personally feel that way — and many people do — that framing tanks the story.

Carry it forward

Everything Contributed to What You Are Becoming

Say that.

Everything you've done contributed to what you're becoming. Say that.

Failure mode three

Expecting the Listener to Make the Connections

They won't.

Third, expecting the listener to make the connections. They won't.

Make the connection visible

Name the Bridge. Explicitly

If your old work relates to your new direction, you have to name the bridge. Explicitly.
