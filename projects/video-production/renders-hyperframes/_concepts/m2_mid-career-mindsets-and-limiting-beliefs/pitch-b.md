# PITCH B — m2_mid-career-mindsets-and-limiting-beliefs

**Lens: accumulation-first.** The build-up was designed before the object was
named. Runtime estimate: ~228 narration words → **~100–110s** of finished cut
(marks below use ~105s; a builder rescales proportionally).

**Fidelity note up front.** Every label on this drawing is a word the script
already says. Every count is a count the script itself enumerates — three
quoted beliefs; three things already built (skills, credibility,
relationships); three named experiments (a leadership role on a project, a
hybrid responsibilities arrangement, an adjacent function with a small
assignment); four re-design levers (projects, boundaries, responsibilities,
skills). **No numbers, no dates, no measurements, no percentages appear
anywhere on the drawing** — the object gains geometry, never data.

---

## The accumulation ladder

What is physically MORE, denser, re-sorted or newly connected at each mark.
The ladder was fixed first; the object was chosen afterwards because it was
the thing that could plausibly do this gaining.

**By ~25s — the frame has gained enclosure.**
Start: a bare pale survey grid, empty, with a single thin open corner mark.
Gained by 25s: **three heavy inked wall segments**, drawn one at a time, each
lettered along its length with one of the script's quoted beliefs; they close
into a **small filled room** with a hatched interior. The room is deliberately
tiny against the grid. Net gain: 0 → 3 heavy walls, an open corner → a sealed
hatched footprint. Density is concentrated in a small central patch; the rest
of the page is still empty. This is the frame's most *cramped* state and the
only one where emptiness dominates.

**By ~50s — the frame has gained ground, and the ground turns out to be
already occupied.**
Gained: a **long-dash chain perimeter** swung wide around the whole page (the
plot the small room was always standing on), **three solid-outline hatched
structures** placed outside the walls — one each for skills, credibility,
relationships, drawn as already-built, already-inked, not new — and
**openings cut into the three belief walls**, each a gap in the ink with a
quarter-circle door-swing arc. Also gained: **tick-ended dimension rules**
running from the room's edge out to the plot line, unlabelled, visibly long.
Net gain: the page goes from one small dense object to a populated site; the
small room is now legibly a fraction of what is already owned. Re-sort: the
three walls stop reading as a container and start reading as a boundary drawn
in the wrong place.

**By ~80s — the frame has gained provisional geometry.**
Gained, in order: **one enormous dashed outline** sketched around the entire
plot (the all-or-nothing pivot), then **struck through and erased back**,
leaving a faint ghost trace — the page keeps the scar. In its place, **three
small dashed annexes** attach to the room, one per opening, each reaching
toward one of the already-built structures, each tagged with a circled letter
and one of the script's own experiment phrases. Annexes are dashed, never
solid: they are proposals, not commitments. Net gain: 0 → 3 attached
provisional volumes plus one erased ghost; the drawing is now busy with two
distinct line weights (built vs proposed) that did not coexist before.

**By ~105s — the frame has gained a finished plan, and re-sorted its own
hierarchy.**
Gained: **four labelled edge-rules** along the drawing's margins —
projects, boundaries, responsibilities, skills — the levers the plan can be
adjusted along. The **three original belief walls are demoted**: their heavy
ink thins to the same dashed weight the proposals had, and their hatching
drains. The perimeter of the lived-in area **expands outward to meet the plot
line**, so the tick-ended dimension rules that were long at 50s are now short.
One annex takes a **solid pin marker** — the one new path. One wall segment
keeps a small **stuck marker**, and a single door swing is drawn open on it,
answering the closing question without answering it for the viewer. Net gain:
the page ends dense edge-to-edge, with the 25s room still visible as a small
hatched core inside it.

## The carrying object

**An architect's floor plan of one person's working role — a single drawing on
a survey grid that begins as one small sealed room and ends as a full site
plan, redrawn outward.**

It is drawn in flat ink, no perspective, plan view only: a 1px pale rule grid
under everything; walls as 6px double lines with 45° diagonal hatch filling
the gap between them; doorways as clean 6px breaks in the wall with a thin
quarter-circle swing arc; already-built structures as solid rectangles with
the same diagonal hatch at half density; proposals as 3px dashed rectangles
with no hatch and a small circled-letter tag at one corner. The site boundary
is a long-dash-dot chain rule. Distance is shown only by thin rules with tick
ends at both extremes — never with a number on them. Colour does exactly one
job: brand ink for anything built, a lighter tint for anything proposed, and
a single accent used exactly twice in the whole runtime (the erased pivot's
strike, and the final pin). Every piece of lettering sits *on* the drawing —
along a wall, beside a structure, in a margin rule — never in a heading bar
above it.

## Four frames

**25% (~26s).** A large pale empty grid. Dead centre and small: a sealed
hatched room, three heavy walls, each carrying one quoted belief in lettering
that runs along the wall. Nothing else on the page. The picture reads as *one
small dense box in a lot of space*.

**50% (~53s).** The page is now a site. A chain-rule perimeter runs near the
frame edges. Three hatched structures stand outside the little room, labelled
skills, credibility, relationships. The room's three walls now have gaps in
them with door-swing arcs. Long tick-ended rules span the gulf between the
room and the perimeter. The picture reads as *a small building on a big plot
that was already built up*.

**75% (~79s).** A faint erased ghost of a single huge dashed outline covers
the whole plot, struck through. Over it, three small dashed annexes hang off
the room's three doorways, each tagged and each pointing at one of the
existing structures. Two line weights fight for the eye. The picture reads as
*a drawing mid-revision, with one big idea crossed out and three small ones
pencilled in*.

**100% (~105s).** A finished plan. Four labelled rules line the margins. The
belief walls are thin and dashed with their hatch drained; the room's core
hatch survives as a small square. The occupied area now runs out to the chain
rule; the tick-ended rules that were long are now short stubs. One annex
carries a solid pin; one wall segment carries a stuck marker with a single
open door swung on it. The picture reads as *a whole site plan you could hand
to someone*.

No two of those are the same picture, and none could be produced by relabelling
another.

---

## The payoff beat

**The close — the pull-back on "acknowledge where you feel stuck, and commit
to exploring one new path."** The drawing stops accumulating and gets read
whole for the first time: the camera widens so the entire plan sits inside the
frame at once, and in that single view the 25s room is visibly a small hatched
square inside a plan that now fills the page. The three belief walls are still
there — thinner, dashed, no longer holding anything — which is what makes the
re-read land: the beliefs were never removed, they were **de-weighted**. The
final two beats add exactly two marks to that whole view: the stuck marker
with its one open door, and the pin on the one experiment. That closing frame
is unproducible at 0:10 — it depends on ink laid down at 25s, 50s and 80s
still being on the page.

---

## Why this is not lazy

A builder cannot satisfy this with a static prop and swapped headings, for
four reasons that are structural rather than stylistic:

1. **The geometry changes state, not just its caption.** The same three wall
   segments must be authored in four distinct renderings — heavy+hatched,
   then breached with door arcs, then annexed, then thinned to dashed with the
   hatch drained. A prop that stays put through the runtime cannot produce
   the 100% frame.
2. **The frame's occupied area moves.** The plot perimeter arrives at ~50s and
   the built area expands out to meet it by ~105s; the tick-ended distance
   rules are long at 50s and short at 105s. That is a spatial relationship
   between two elements, so it cannot be faked with text.
3. **New elements attach to specific earlier elements.** Each dashed annex
   hangs off a particular doorway cut into a particular belief wall and points
   at a particular already-built structure. The connections only exist
   because the earlier beats put those anchors on the page — the drawing has
   a dependency graph, and heading swaps have none.
4. **The page keeps its history.** The erased all-or-nothing pivot leaves a
   ghost; the original room survives as a hatched core. A viewer given the
   contact sheet shuffled could restore the order from the ink alone, which
   is exactly the test the thin rejected cut failed.

Beat granularity supports the pace floor without splitting: each wall (3),
the perimeter, each existing structure (3), the door-cutting, the big pivot
sketched, the big pivot struck, each annex (3), the four margin rules, the
wall demotion, the pull-back, the two closing marks — roughly 17–18 beats of
genuine new ink across ~105s, every one of them adding or re-weighting
geometry rather than replacing a line of text.

**Ceiling on interpretation:** nothing on this drawing may assert a fact the
narration does not. No square footage, no timeline, no before/after metric, no
named employer, no count beyond the script's own enumerations. If a beat wants
a number, the beat is wrong.
