## The accumulation ladder

What the frame has physically **gained** at each mark — more elements, more
density, a different sort order, or a new connection. Every gain traces to a
line the script actually speaks; nothing here counts, dates, or sequences
anything the narration does not.

**By 0:30 — gained a second column, one deformed mark, and the first
unreadable region.**
The frame opens on a bare ruled working surface with a single short column of
tally marks. By 0:30 there are **two** columns (the pro and con list), and one
mark inside them has grown physically oversized — twice the stroke weight and
height of its neighbours, shouldering them apart. That deformity is the whole
of "easy to give too much weight to an unimportant factor," drawn rather than
labelled. Simultaneously a stippled grey field has crept in from the right
edge: the first thing on the frame a viewer **cannot read**. Net gain: +1
column, +1 deformed element, +1 illegible region. Total ink still low.

**By 1:00 — gained scale, loose unanchored marks, and the first permanent
structural feature.**
The camera has pulled back and the two columns are now revealed as small
against a much larger board — the picture got *bigger*, not busier, which is
what "you can do much better" looks like before any better thing exists.
Scattered short marks have appeared outside both columns, anchored to nothing,
drifting at slight angles (most people use no process; going with your gut).
And the board's left rail gains its first **drilled hole** — small, dark,
permanent, unexplained. Net gain: +1 revealed canvas, +a scatter of unanchored
marks, +1 hole in the rail.

**By 1:30 — gained an edge, a dated tag, and the first standing pins.**
A boundary is scored into the surface around the working area, and a small tag
is clipped to its top-right corner carrying a date field (clarify exactly what
you are choosing, **and by when**). The loose drifting marks are swept: some
inside the boundary, some off the board entirely. Two or three **pins** now
stand up out of the surface where flat tally marks used to lie — the frame's
first elements with height. The rail gains a second hole. Net gain: +1 scored
boundary, +1 dated tag, +3 standing objects, −the drift, +1 hole.

**By 2:00 — gained population, past the comfortable margin, in two visible
waves.**
Pins multiply. They arrive in a first wave that fills the boundary, then —
after a beat's pause — a second wave and a third that overrun the boundary's
comfortable inner margin and crowd its edge ("push yourself to list more paths
than feels comfortable"; "ask what else at least twice" is the two extra
waves, and nothing on frame ever prints a number). The board is now visibly
dense with **identical, bare, undifferentiated** pins — deliberately
undifferentiated, because they have not been rated yet. Rail: third hole. Net
gain: a population you cannot count at a glance, +1 hole.

**By 2:30 — gained internal detail, a wholesale re-sort, and one patch of
legibility bought back.**
Each pin grows a small **punched strip** beneath it — a short horizontal band
with between three and seven punch positions, each one filled or blank (the
three to seven factors that truly matter). The board then **re-sorts**: pins
travel once, into ranked rows, so the arrangement a viewer memorised at 2:00 is
gone and the strips step down the frame in a readable gradient. Then one single
thread runs out from a high-ranked pin into the grey stipple and back — a short
there-and-back excursion, the cheap experiment — and inside the small window
that thread enclosed, the stipple resolves into crisp, readable surface. The
rest of the grey stays grey, permanently, because the script says you rarely
need certainty. Net gain: +1 strip per pin, +a new sort order, +1 thread, +1
cleared window, +1 hole.

**By 3:10 (end) — gained one committed inked line, a hanging review tag, and a
reusable jig.**
One thread is drawn taut from the start peg on the rail to a single pin and
inked solid — heavier than every other line on the board. A second small tag
hangs from that thread with a date field on it (set a date to check back in).
Nothing is erased: every other pin still stands, every strip still readable,
the grey still grey. Then the loose threads lift, the pins are drawn out, and
what remains is the scored boundary and **five holes in a clean vertical
column** down the left rail — punched one per step, on the way past, since 1:00
— now reading for the first time as the process itself, empty and ready to be
strung again. Net gain: +1 inked commitment, +1 review tag, and the whole run
re-read as a tool.

## The carrying object

**A peg-and-thread decision board** — a dark, ruled workshop board where every
option a person is considering is a brass pin pushed into it, every factor is a
punch in the small strip under that pin, and the decision itself is one taut
inked thread from the rail to one pin.

It is drawn as flat geometry with a single light source: the board is a
full-bleed rectangle in the deep brand ground with a faint square rule; a pin
is a filled circle with a short offset ellipse under it for the shaft's shadow,
in the brand accent; a punched strip is a rounded rectangle outline holding
three to seven small squares, filled or hollow. Threads are 3–4px strokes with
one soft bend, the committed one at double weight; the boundary is a scored
inset rectangle with corner ticks; the tags are small rectangles with a
punched-corner notch and a ruled date line. Uncertainty is a stipple field —
irregular dots at varying opacity — and clearing it means replacing stipple
with clean rule, so "cleared" is a measurable ink change, not a glow. No hex is
quoted here: ground, accent and neutral come from the workspace's copied
`tokens.yml`. Pins enter once and never move again except in the single
2:15-ish re-sort transit; nothing wobbles, breathes, or re-marks after it
settles.

## Four frames

**25% — 0:47** (end of the reality check, entering "most people use no
process"). A large empty ruled board. Slightly left of centre, two short
columns of flat tally marks, small in the frame, with one mark in the left
column grossly oversized and crowding its neighbours out of alignment. The
right third of the board is grey stipple, densest at the edge, thinning inward.
Three or four short marks float outside both columns at wrong angles, touching
nothing. No pins, no boundary, no threads. **Reads as:** a small flat list,
adrift, with one thing wrongly enormous and a whole region nobody can see into.

**50% — 1:34** (step one done, step two starting). A scored boundary now frames
the working area; a dated tag is clipped to its top-right corner. The tally
columns are gone. Inside the boundary stand **three** pins, well spaced, with
lots of clean board around them; a fourth is entering at the margin. Two holes
sit in the left rail. The grey stipple is unchanged on the right. **Reads as:**
a clean, structured, deliberately under-populated board — order arrived before
material did.

**75% — 2:22** (mid step four, the experiment running). The board is crowded:
pins in ranked rows across most of the surface, each with its punched strip
beneath, the filled/hollow squares stepping down row by row so the sort order
is visible from across the room. Several pins sit outside the boundary's inner
margin — the overrun from widening, never tidied away. One thread arcs from a
top row out into the stipple and back; inside the loop it made, one small
window of the grey has become crisp ruled surface, hard-edged against the
stipple around it. **Reads as:** a dense, instrumented, sorted board with one
bright hole cut in the fog.

**100% — 3:10** (closing). Two-part frame. First: everything at once, pulled
back — boundary, dated tag, the full population, the ranked strips, the cleared
window, and one heavy inked thread running from the rail's start peg to a
single pin with a review tag hanging off it. Then the strip-back: threads lift,
pins withdraw, and the held final image is the scored boundary plus **five
holes in a vertical column** down the left rail, one fresh pin waiting at the
top. **Reads as:** the tool that did all of that, empty, ready for the next
one.

## The payoff beat

**2:50–3:07, on "Because this is a loop and not a one-time event… and once you
know how to make one good decision, you can use that same skill again and
again."**

Two moves, in that order. **The whole run becomes visible at once** — the pull
back at 2:50 is the first frame in the video that holds every accumulated layer
in one picture: the boundary and its date, the overrun population, the ranked
strips, the cleared window in the grey, and the single inked line. That frame
is unreachable at 0:10 by construction; at 0:10 the board holds one column of
tally marks.

Then **the object re-reads as a jig**: the threads lift and the pins withdraw,
and the five rail holes — punched one at a time, on the way past, at 1:00,
1:30, 2:00, 2:30 and 2:50, never remarked on when they appeared — resolve into
the process itself. The re-read is earned rather than announced, because the
viewer watched every hole get punched without being told what it was for. The
board can be strung again for which internship, which major, which offer, which
next move, and that is the last thing the frame shows.

## Why this is not lazy

**A heading swap cannot move between any two adjacent rungs of the ladder.**
Every rung is a change in the count, geometry, or legibility of drawn elements,
not a change in words:

- **0:30 → 1:00** requires the canvas to be re-scaled and a scatter of
  unanchored marks authored at individual angles. No text change produces a
  smaller-looking list.
- **1:30 → 2:00** requires the pin population to grow from three to a field, in
  three authored waves with a beat between them, some of them placed *outside*
  the boundary. A static prop cannot gain objects.
- **2:00 → 2:30** requires a per-pin sub-element (the punched strip, with its
  own filled/hollow states) and then a **wholesale positional re-sort** — every
  pin at a new coordinate. This is the single hardest thing in the concept to
  fake and the easiest to verify: the shuffle test passes because the 2:00 and
  2:30 arrangements cannot be confused.
- **2:30 → 3:10** requires a stroke-weight change on exactly one thread, a new
  hanging tag, and then subtraction — the removal of everything except the
  boundary and the rail. A concept whose final move is *removal* cannot be
  satisfied by adding a heading.

**It also cannot be satisfied by a small prop on empty space** (the thin-cut
failure). The board is the frame — full-bleed — and it is monotonically fuller
from 1:30 to 2:50, so "is the carrier earning its canvas" is answered by
construction rather than by discipline.

**Milestone contract for the builder** (the hole taste.md says CONCEPT.md must
close): at 1:34 exactly three pins and zero strips; at 2:22 a pin field with a
strip under every pin and a visible rank gradient; at 2:50 all layers present
simultaneously; at 3:07 pins and threads gone, five rail holes present. Any
build that reaches the gate with the same pin count at 1:34 and 2:22 has failed
the concept, not the gate.

**Not the sibling's device.** Nothing here weighs, tips, balances, or compares
two sides — the criteria beat is a *punch card*, a present/absent record under
each option, and the sort it produces is spatial ranking, not a beam finding
level. The only two-sided figure in the video is the pro-and-con list at the
top, and the whole point of that image is that it is deformed and gets
abandoned by 1:30.

**Fidelity note.** Every element traces to spoken narration: two columns and
one oversized mark (pro/con lists overweight an unimportant factor), the
stipple that never fully clears (you rarely need certainty), the boundary and
its dated tag (clarify what you are choosing, and by when), three waves of pins
(ask what else at least twice), three-to-seven punch positions (the three to
seven factors), one out-and-back thread (one cheap experiment on the biggest
uncertainty), one inked line plus a review tag (decide, commit, set a date),
five rail holes (steps one to five), and the empty strung jig (a repeatable
loop you can run for any choice). No number is ever printed on frame.
