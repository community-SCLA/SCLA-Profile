# PITCH A — do-not-just-ask-what-ai-replaces_early-career-boost

**Lens:** metaphor-first — one concrete object that carries the whole runtime and visibly accumulates.

---

## The carrying object

**A workbench with a pegboard tool wall behind it: the tools hanging on the board are the tasks of a role, and over the lesson the board is re-hung — never emptied — into a machine side and a human side, with bare pegs opening between them.**

Drawn as flat brand-mark line work, not rendering: the board is a large rounded rectangle filled with a regular grid of small hollow circles (peg holes), each hung tool a single-weight silhouette on a short hook — a pen, a ruler, a stamp, a clipboard, a magnifier, a telephone handset, a pair of clasped hands, a set of scales. Below it a single horizontal slab is the bench top, with one flat sheet of work lying on it and a simple open hand at the left edge; a jointed mechanical arm reaches in from the right, three straight segments and a two-finger grip. Weight is the only encoding that changes: a tool gains value by growing in scale and taking a heavier outline plus a fill; a tool moves to the machine side by shrinking to a thin outline with a small gear notch on its hook. No shading, no perspective — everything reads at a glance in silhouette.

---

## Four frames

**25% — "the board, crowded and unsorted, under a shadow."**
Wide on the whole tool wall. Every peg is hung, tools jammed edge to edge in no order, all drawn at the same weight. A single hard-edged shadow — one plain angled shape, no face, no figure — falls across the left third of the board. One line of copy carries the question the script names. Nothing has moved yet; the board is at its maximum crowding and its minimum organisation, and that is the picture: a role you cannot yet read.

**50% — "one task, up close, changes hands."**
The camera is in tight on a four-by-three patch of pegs; the rest of the board is a pale ghost at the frame edges. The mechanical arm's grip has lifted ONE tool — the pen — clear of its hook and holds it out to the right. The hook it left is a bare circle with nothing on it. The three tools immediately around that bare hook have grown to roughly double their earlier size, taken the heavy outline and picked up short labels drawn from the script's own words for this example. This is a detail frame, not a wall frame: the same object, a different distance, a different fact.

**75% — "the bench, running."**
The camera drops to the bench top and the board sits behind it, now visibly split by one drawn vertical rule into a thin-outline column on the right and a heavy-outline column on the left. On the bench a single sheet travels a closed rectangular circuit with four marked stops: it leaves the open hand, crosses to the mechanical grip, comes back to the hand, and arrives at a fourth stop where it lies flat and finished. Only the sheet is in motion; the board holds still, changed and readable behind it.

**100% — "the whole wall, re-hung."**
Back to the wide, and it cannot be mistaken for the 25% frame. Same tools, none deleted — every one still hanging, but sorted: thin-outline tools clustered right of the rule, heavy-outline tools massed left, the left column plainly taller and denser. The bare pegs that opened during the middle of the lesson are the brightest thing on the board, and three of them now carry newly hung tools drawn in the heaviest weight of all, labelled with the three things the closing line says you can decide to build. On the bench below, the finished sheet stays where the loop left it.

---

## What accumulates

Five things are more, re-arranged, or resolved at the end than at the start, and every one of them is visible in a still:

1. **A sorting rule that did not exist.** The board starts with no structure; one vertical division line is drawn once around the middle of the lesson and is never redrawn — from then on every tool has a side.
2. **Weight differential.** Tools begin at uniform weight. By the end one column is thin-outline and one is heavy-outline-and-filled, so the wall has a visible centre of mass it did not have at 0:10.
3. **Openings — a new kind of mark.** Bare pegs simply do not exist in the opening frames. They appear one at a time as tools move, and they are the literal drawing of the word the script uses for them.
4. **Three tools that were never on the board.** The final beats hang new silhouettes on the openings. The board ends with more hung tools than the drafting example left it with.
5. **A finished piece of work.** The bench starts with a blank sheet and ends with a marked-up one that came off the loop.

None of this is a moving highlight. Freeze any two frames far apart and the count of bare pegs, the presence of the division line, and the outline weight of any named tool tell you which came first.

---

## The payoff beat

**The closing wide, on "look for the openings instead of the threats."** The whole wall comes back into frame for the first time since the opening, and it re-reads: nothing was thrown away — every tool that was hanging at 25% is still hanging — but the board is now organised, the human column carries the weight, and the gaps that opened along the way are the only places anything new got hung. The shadow that fell across the left third in the opening frame is gone, and the heavy column occupies exactly the space it covered. That frame is impossible to draw at 0:10: it requires the division line, the migrations and the openings to have already happened on screen.

---

## Why this is not lazy

The board's state at any beat is the *sum of every beat before it*, so there is no static prop to swap headings against. The contract a builder has to hit is positional, not decorative: at the drafting example a specific tool must be in the grip and its hook must be bare; the division line must be drawn exactly once and never again; each named durable skill must arrive as a weight change on a tool that is already on the wall, not as a new card; the three closing tools must be hung on pegs the audience watched open. A heading swap cannot produce a bare peg, and it cannot make one column heavier than the other.

It also forbids the thin-cut failure by construction. The lesson visits the same object at three genuinely different distances — full wall, a tight patch of pegs, and the bench below — so the concept cannot be satisfied by one small prop sitting in the middle of empty frames. And it forbids the slow-cut failure too: because progress is recorded as accumulated marks rather than replaced pictures, the board can absorb a new idea every few seconds without the frame churning, which is the shape both approved reference cuts have.

**Fidelity note.** Every label on frame is lifted from the narration's own words; nothing states a count of tasks, a percentage, an order of events the script does not give, or any claim about what AI will or will not do beyond what the script says. The starting board is deliberately drawn with unlabelled tool silhouettes so that no number of tasks is ever asserted — a tool gets a name only at the moment the narration says it.
