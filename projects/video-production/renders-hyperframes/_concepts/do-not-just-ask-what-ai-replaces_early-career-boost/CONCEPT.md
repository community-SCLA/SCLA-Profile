# CONCEPT — do-not-just-ask-what-ai-replaces_early-career-boost

**Judged:** 2026-08-05, concept-judge stage (B2). Bracket: `design-system/docs/taste.md`,
two APPROVED rows only. Winner: **Pitch B** (accumulation-first). Runner-up: Pitch A
(pegboard tool wall).

Working runtime ≈ **190s** (~470 narration words at the pinned voice + 1.8s final hold).
All clock times below are derived from that estimate; if the synthesised narration comes
in longer or shorter, hold the **percentages**, not the timecodes.

---

## Concept angle

**One slab of masonry labelled with a single role, broken into its task-bricks and
re-laid — never emptied — into a doorway with a clear opening in it, with a hard-edged
shadow lying across the frame that retreats as the wall is re-laid**: the wall is on
screen from the title beat to the final beat (0:00 → end, ~100% of runtime), and all of
its material exists by ~0:30 (≈16%), so everything after that is re-arrangement, not
arrival.

---

## The carrying object

A **wall of rectangular task-bricks** and **one hard-edged angled shadow**.

**The bricks.** Flat rectangles only — 4px corner radius, 2px stroke, near-transparent
fill, heights roughly 28–72px, widths roughly 90–260px, separated by a uniform 6px mortar
gap. Widths are **irregular** and rows are laid in **running bond** (each course offset
against the one below), so the field reads as masonry texture and never as a stack of
equal rows. There are no icons, no gradients, no shadows on the bricks themselves and no
second illustration anywhere on the frame: the masonry *is* the hero illustration, and it
uses the full canvas from ~0:30 onward.

**Weight is the only encoding.** A brick that becomes less scarce loses height and
stretches wide into a thin strip. A brick that becomes more valuable grows taller and
picks up a second stroke. Those two silhouettes — thin-and-wide versus tall-and-double —
must be tellable apart at contact-sheet thumbnail size with every label hidden.

**The datum** is a single 2px rule spanning most of the frame width, drawn once and never
redrawn. **The loop path** is a 3px polyline with four small square nodes. **The opening
slab** (beat 1 only) is one large rectangle carrying 2px dashed internal scoring — the
cut-lines of a thing about to be demolished, which is the "will AI take my job?" reading.

**The shadow** is one plain hard-edged angled quadrilateral in a darker tint of the
background — **no face, no figure, no hand, nothing representational**. It lies across the
left third at the open, and bricks inside it are drawn at reduced opacity. It only ever
retreats. It is not a mood effect; it is a measurable region of the frame that shrinks
monotonically, and it is the one element that is allowed to change without a brick moving.

**Motion discipline.** A brick performs exactly one action per beat — one lay, one
migration, one thickening — and is pixel-static afterwards. The shadow retreats at most
once per beat and is pixel-static between. Nothing re-animates in place, ever. One brick
action = one idea = one beat is the pace atom that carries ≥8 beats/min without splitting
anything.

## Milestone frames — the ACCUMULATION CONTRACT

Each of these must be a genuinely different **picture**. The build check is the
**hidden-label silhouette test**: cover every word on all four frames and they must still
be four distinguishable images.

**25% (~0:47) — a scatter above an empty line, under a shadow.**
No stacking anywhere. Fifteen to twenty loose bricks drift across the upper two-thirds at
mixed angles, **unlabelled**, one held clearly apart from the rest. The original slab is
gone as a shape but wholly present as its pieces. A single thin horizontal rule is being
drawn across the lower third with **nothing on it yet**. The shadow still lies across the
left third; the bricks inside it are dimmer than their neighbours.
*Silhouette: scatter.*

**50% (~1:35) — a low flat strip, a short thick row, and one traveller.**
Along the bottom, a wide flat band of thin strips. Above the datum, a short but tall row
of double-stroked bricks carrying four labels — judgment, relationships, creativity,
oversight. Dead centre, one brick mid-migration between the two courses: the only unsettled
thing on frame. The shadow has retreated off the upper course entirely and now covers only
the lower-left strips.
*Silhouette: two courses of obviously different heights, same material.*

**75% (~2:22) — a dense two-tone wall with a line beginning to cross it.**
The upper course is at full height, edge to edge, and fully lettered with the durable set
the narration names. The lower strip is fully lettered and unmistakably thinner than
anything above it. The first leg of the connecting path has left the top course and is
descending through a mortar gap toward the strip. This is the densest the frame ever gets;
every later gain is structural, not textual. The shadow is a thin sliver at the extreme
left edge.
*Silhouette: stratified wall, one line crossing.*

**100% (~3:10) — an aperture, not a wall.**
Two piers, a three-brick lintel (judgment / range / tool fluency), the thin strip as a
threshold, and the closed loop path now running around the doorframe. Between the piers is
a clear rectangular void — **positioned exactly where the shadow lay in the opening
frame**, and the shadow is gone. This is the widest and tallest silhouette in the video,
and every piece that was in the opening slab is still visibly on screen, just re-laid.
*Silhouette: aperture.*

Four silhouettes: **solid rectangle → scatter → stratified wall → aperture.** The shuffle
test is passable from the pictures alone.

## What accumulates

1. **Edges.** The frame opens as one unbroken solid; by ~0:30 the dashed scoring has been
   re-drawn as real joints and the solid has come apart into discrete pieces. Many outlines
   where there was one. Nothing leaves the frame — that is load-bearing for the whole run.
2. **A sort and a datum.** One horizontal rule is drawn once, and from then on every brick
   has a side: below it the work that gets done faster or cheaper, above it the work that
   becomes more valuable as a result.
3. **A thickness differential.** Bricks begin at uniform weight. By 50% one course is thin
   and wide and one is tall and double-stroked, so the wall has a visible centre of mass it
   did not have at 0:10.
4. **Lettering, to a fixed budget.** Labels arrive only as the narration says the word, and
   maximum ink is reached at ~75%. After that the frame gains no new text at all.
5. **Connections.** For the first time in the runtime a line crosses between the courses —
   a four-stop path: human question, AI draft, human review, better result. Before this
   beat the two courses were a comparison; after it they are one working thing.
6. **An opening, and the largest silhouette of the video.** The wall's densest region
   becomes a void with a lintel over it.
7. **Retreat of the shadow.** A monotonically shrinking dark region: left third → lower-left
   strips only → a sliver at the edge → gone.

Freeze any two frames far apart and the count of settled bricks, the presence of the datum,
the height of any named brick, and the extent of the shadow tell you which came first.

---

## The payoff beat

**"Look for the openings instead of the threats" (~2:55).** The upper course splits and
moves outward into two piers, the lintel drops in, and the void appears. The re-read is the
whole argument in one move: no brick left the frame, so the opening was not made by anything
being *deleted* — it was made by the same pieces being re-laid. That is the script's own
line, "most roles are not deleted — they are reshaped," restated as geometry, and it is only
legible because the viewer watched every brick arrive.

The **final beat** ("curiosity beats dread") gets one legal move and one only: the last
sliver of shadow clears out through the void, leaving the aperture as the brightest region
of the frame. Then it holds, pixel-static, through the 1.8s final hold.

A second, smaller re-read runs underneath: the **judgment** brick is laid at ~1:00,
thickened at ~2:00, and ends up in the lintel at ~3:10 — one piece with a three-act life a
viewer can trace backwards.

The closing frame is impossible to draw at 0:10. It requires the break-up, the datum, the
thickness split and the path to have already happened on screen.

---

## Grafted from the runner-up

**Pitch A's hard-edged shadow — the angled shape with no face and no figure that falls
across the opening frame and is gone by the close, its old ground occupied by the thing the
lesson built.**

Two reasons it earns its place in B. First, B's opening (a solid with demolition scoring)
stated the dread but gave it no *object*, so nothing in the frame could resolve it; the
script explicitly names the feeling ("it can also feel unsettling", "you do not need to
panic", "curiosity beats dread"), and a shrinking measurable region is that feeling drawn
rather than narrated. Second, and more practically, B's final beat as pitched was a static
hold on a finished doorframe — a dead last beat, which is exactly the shape this pipeline
keeps getting caught on. Landing the void where the shadow lay converts the last beat from
a hold into a re-read, at the cost of one element.

Pitch A's other strong idea — visiting the object at three different camera distances — was
deliberately **not** grafted. B already earns four distinct silhouettes from the object
itself, and a tight crop would shrink the carrier's share of the canvas, which is the exact
failure of the rejected thin cut.

---

## Judge's note

**The failure mode this concept will slide into: the two courses degenerating into two
bulleted lists in rounded rectangles.** At ~75% both courses are fully lettered, and a
builder under time pressure will reach for the pipeline's default card form — equal-width
rows, one stacked above the other, aligned left, a heading over each. At that point the
object has stopped being an object and become typography, the wall is a comparison table in
costume, and the concept fails the same way the thin cut did: legal on every gate, nothing
to look at.

**The concrete guard, three parts, all checkable before render:**

1. **Hidden-label silhouette test.** Take the four milestone snapshots, hide every text
   node, and confirm they are still four distinguishable pictures. If two match with the
   words off, the beats between them are dead and must be re-authored.
2. **Masonry, not rows.** No two bricks in a course share a width; every course is laid in
   running bond with its neighbours' joints offset; no brick carries more than three words;
   every label is a noun the narration actually speaks. No course is ever drawn as a
   left-aligned stack of equal-width rows.
3. **No enumeration, ever.** No count, no numbering, no total on frame, in any course. The
   script never says how many tasks a role has, and a numbered brick field asserts it.

## Scores

**Pitch A — pegboard tool wall + workbench.** *Name the object:* strong — a peg wall is
concrete and nameable, and bare pegs are a genuinely good literal drawing of the script's
word "openings". *Four-frame test:* passes, but two of its four frames are the same wide
shot differentiated by content rather than shape, and the 50% frame buys its difference with
a camera crop rather than with the object. *What accumulates:* five real, still-visible
gains — the sorting rule, the weight differential, the bare pegs, the new tools, the finished
sheet. *Payoff:* good — the closing wide re-reads and the shadow's old ground is taken by the
human column. *Lazy-build test:* mostly closed, with one hole: at 75% the carrier is demoted
to a background element behind a bench, and "the rest of the board is a pale ghost at the
frame edges" at 50% gives a tired builder cover to draw very little. Two harder problems
decide it. First, the concept needs eight or more *recognisable* tool silhouettes — a
telephone handset, a pair of scales, clasped hands — drawn flat and legible at
contact-sheet size; built cheaply, that collapses into a grid of identical small circles,
which is the rejected thin cut in a new costume. Second, against the approved bracket it has
the wrong shape: both APPROVED rows put all their material on screen early and then only
ever re-read or re-group it, while A hangs three brand-new tools in the closing beats.

**Pitch B — task-bricks re-laid into a doorway.** *Name the object:* a labelled slab broken
into bricks is concrete and, more importantly, buildable in this pipeline from flat
rectangles alone — there is no drawing skill to fail at, so density is the only deliverable.
*Four-frame test:* the strongest answer of the two, and the only one whose four frames differ
in **silhouette** rather than in content — solid, scatter, stratified wall, aperture — which
is precisely what a contact sheet at thumbnail size can adjudicate. *What accumulates:* a
six-rung ladder in which every rung is geometry, three of them change no copy at all, and
maximum ink lands at 75% so the last quarter is structural. *Payoff:* the best single beat in
either pitch — the void that appears without a brick leaving the frame is the script's own
"not deleted — reshaped" drawn rather than said. *Lazy-build test:* closed by construction —
brick conservation constrains element identity rather than copy, the copy budget is fixed by
the narration so there are no spare headings to swap, and an aperture cannot be produced by
relabelling a wall. Decisively, B matches the APPROVED signature exactly: all its material
exists by ~0:30, and every beat after that re-groups what is already on screen — the same
shape as the 48-mark field and the built-early-then-re-read map. Its real risk is textural
(masonry reading as an enumeration, courses reading as bulleted lists), which is guardable
before render; A's risk is representational, and only visible after someone has drawn eight
icons badly.

**Verdict: PITCH B wins**, with Pitch A's retreating shadow grafted in to give the opening
frame an antagonist and the closing beat a re-read instead of a static hold.
